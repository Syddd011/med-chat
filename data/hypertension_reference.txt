HYPERTENSION (HIGH BLOOD PRESSURE) — MEDICAL REFERENCE

DEFINITION
Hypertension, also known as high blood pressure, is a condition in which the force of the blood against the artery walls is consistently too high. Blood pressure is measured using two numbers: systolic (pressure when the heart beats) and diastolic (pressure when the heart rests between beats). Normal blood pressure is less than 120/80 mmHg. High blood pressure is defined as 130/80 mmHg or higher.

CLASSIFICATION
- Normal: Less than 120/80 mmHg
- Elevated: Systolic 120-129 and diastolic less than 80 mmHg
- Stage 1 Hypertension: Systolic 130-139 or diastolic 80-89 mmHg
- Stage 2 Hypertension: Systolic 140 or higher or diastolic 90 mmHg or higher
- Hypertensive Crisis: Systolic higher than 180 and/or diastolic higher than 120 mmHg — requires emergency medical care

CAUSES AND RISK FACTORS
Primary (essential) hypertension has no identifiable cause and develops gradually over years. Secondary hypertension is caused by underlying conditions including kidney disease, thyroid problems, obstructive sleep apnea, adrenal gland tumors, and certain medications such as birth control pills, cold remedies, decongestants, over-the-counter pain relievers, and some prescription drugs.

Risk factors include: age (risk increases with age), race (more common in Black adults), family history, being overweight or obese, physical inactivity, tobacco use, too much salt (sodium) in diet, too little potassium in diet, drinking too much alcohol, stress, and certain chronic conditions such as kidney disease, diabetes, and sleep apnea.

SYMPTOMS
Hypertension is often called the "silent killer" because most people with high blood pressure have no symptoms. Some people may experience headaches, shortness of breath, or nosebleeds, but these symptoms typically do not occur until blood pressure has reached a severe or life-threatening stage.

COMPLICATIONS
Untreated hypertension can damage the heart, arteries, kidneys, brain, and eyes. Complications include: heart attack, heart failure, stroke, aneurysm, kidney scarring or failure, vision loss, memory problems and dementia, metabolic syndrome, and sexual dysfunction.

DIAGNOSIS
Blood pressure is measured with a blood pressure cuff (sphygmomanometer). Readings should be taken on two separate occasions. A 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitor may be used for a more accurate diagnosis.

TREATMENT
Lifestyle changes are the first line of treatment. These include: eating a heart-healthy diet (DASH diet) with less salt, regular physical activity (at least 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic exercise), maintaining a healthy weight, limiting alcohol, not smoking, reducing stress, monitoring blood pressure at home.

Medications used to treat high blood pressure include: 
- Diuretics (water pills): thiazide diuretics, chlorthalidone
- ACE inhibitors: lisinopril, enalapril, ramipril
- Angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs): losartan, valsartan, olmesartan
- Calcium channel blockers: amlodipine, diltiazem, nifedipine
- Beta-blockers: metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol
- Alpha-blockers
- Alpha-beta blockers

PREVENTION
Adopt a healthy lifestyle including regular exercise, a balanced low-sodium diet, maintaining a healthy weight, limiting alcohol consumption, not smoking, managing stress, and getting regular medical check-ups.

WHEN TO SEE A DOCTOR
See a doctor if you are consistently getting blood pressure readings of 130/80 or higher, or if you experience severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, or vision changes — these can indicate a hypertensive crisis requiring emergency care.
