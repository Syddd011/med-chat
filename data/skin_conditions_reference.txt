SKIN CONDITIONS — MEDICAL REFERENCE

ECZEMA (ATOPIC DERMATITIS)
A chronic, inflammatory skin condition causing dry, itchy, inflamed skin. Very common — affects 10–20% of children and 1–3% of adults.

CAUSES AND TRIGGERS
Genetic predisposition (often runs in families with asthma, hay fever). Skin barrier defect allows moisture to escape and irritants/allergens to enter.
Common triggers: dry skin, soaps and detergents, sweat, stress, certain foods (in some patients), dust mites, pet dander, synthetic fabrics, cold weather, fragrances.

SYMPTOMS
Dry, sensitive skin. Intense itching (especially at night). Red to brownish-grey patches. Small, raised bumps that may weep fluid when scratched. Thickened, cracked, or scaly skin. Swollen skin from scratching.
Most commonly affects: inner elbows, backs of knees, hands, feet, face (especially in infants — cheeks, scalp).

TREATMENT
Skin care: Moisturize immediately after bathing (within 3 minutes). Use thick creams or ointments (petroleum jelly, ceramide-based moisturizers). Avoid soap — use gentle, fragrance-free cleansers. Bathe in lukewarm (not hot) water.
Topical corticosteroids: Reduce inflammation during flare-ups. Hydrocortisone (1%) for mild cases. Stronger steroids (betamethasone, clobetasol) for severe flares under doctor's supervision.
Topical calcineurin inhibitors: Tacrolimus (Protopic), pimecrolimus (Elidel) — non-steroid option, especially for face and skin folds.
Antihistamines: For itching, especially at night. Sedating ones (diphenhydramine) can help with sleep.
Wet wrap therapy: For severe eczema in children.
Biologics: Dupilumab (Dupixent) — for moderate-severe eczema in adults not responding to conventional treatment.
Antibiotics: If secondary bacterial infection develops (Staphylococcus aureus is common).

PSORIASIS
A chronic autoimmune condition that speeds up the skin cell life cycle, causing cells to build up rapidly on the surface.

TYPES
Plaque psoriasis (most common): Raised, red patches covered with thick, silvery scales. Usually on elbows, knees, scalp, lower back.
Guttate psoriasis: Small drop-shaped lesions on trunk/limbs. Often triggered by streptococcal infection.
Pustular psoriasis: White pustules surrounded by red skin.
Erythrodermic psoriasis: Inflammatory type covering large body surface area — can be life-threatening.
Nail psoriasis: Pitting, thickening, discoloration of nails.
Psoriatic arthritis: Joint pain and swelling (affects up to 30% of people with psoriasis).

TRIGGERS
Stress, skin injury (Koebner phenomenon), infections, certain medications (lithium, beta-blockers), smoking, alcohol, obesity.

TREATMENT
Topical: Corticosteroids, vitamin D analogs (calcipotriol), coal tar, salicylic acid, calcineurin inhibitors.
Phototherapy: UVB (narrow-band UVB), PUVA (psoralen + UVA).
Systemic (for moderate-severe): Methotrexate, cyclosporine, acitretin.
Biologics: TNF inhibitors (adalimumab, etanercept), IL-17 inhibitors (secukinumab, ixekizumab), IL-23 inhibitors (guselkumab, risankizumab) — highly effective.

ACNE VULGARIS
Very common skin condition affecting hair follicles and sebaceous glands. Affects 85% of people aged 12–25.

CAUSES
Excess sebum production, clogging of hair follicles, Cutibacterium acnes (bacteria), inflammation. Hormonal factors (androgens increase sebum production). Worsened by: stress, certain cosmetics, dietary factors (some evidence for high glycemic diet and dairy).

TYPES OF LESIONS
Non-inflammatory: Blackheads (open comedones), whiteheads (closed comedones).
Inflammatory: Papules (red bumps), pustules (pimples), nodules, cysts. Severe nodular/cystic acne can cause scarring.

TREATMENT (based on severity)
Mild: Topical benzoyl peroxide (antibacterial), topical retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin), topical antibiotics (clindamycin).
Moderate: Combination topical therapy + oral antibiotics (doxycycline, tetracycline for 3 months maximum).
Severe / Cystic: Oral isotretinoin (Accutane) — very effective but requires monitoring (birth defects risk — pregnancy must be avoided). For women: oral contraceptives, spironolactone.
General tips: Wash face twice daily with gentle cleanser. Don't pick or pop pimples. Use non-comedogenic (won't clog pores) moisturizers and sunscreen.

FUNGAL SKIN INFECTIONS

DERMATOPHYTOSIS (RINGWORM / TINEA)
Not a worm — caused by fungi. Named by location:
Tinea corporis (ringworm) — ring-shaped rash on body.
Tinea capitis — scalp (common in children, can cause hair loss).
Tinea pedis (Athlete's foot) — between/under toes; itching, scaling, blistering.
Tinea unguium (Onychomycosis) — nail infection; thickened, discolored, brittle nails.
Tinea cruris (Jock itch) — groin, inner thighs; red, itchy rash.

TREATMENT
Topical antifungals: Clotrimazole, miconazole, terbinafine — for most tinea infections. Apply 2 weeks beyond apparent cure.
Oral antifungals: Terbinafine, itraconazole, griseofulvin — for nail infections, scalp ringworm, widespread or resistant infections.

CANDIDIASIS (YEAST INFECTION OF SKIN)
Caused by Candida (yeast). Common in warm, moist areas: skin folds, under breasts, groin, armpits, between fingers/toes.
Appears as: red, intensely itchy rash with satellite lesions (small spots around main rash). Can have white coating in mouth (oral thrush) or diaper area (infants).
Treatment: Topical antifungal cream (clotrimazole, nystatin). Oral fluconazole for widespread/persistent infection. Keep area clean and dry.

URTICARIA (HIVES)
Raised, itchy welts on skin. Can appear anywhere. Individual hives usually disappear within 24 hours but new ones can keep appearing.
Causes: allergic reactions (food, medication, insect sting), infection, stress, heat, pressure, idiopathic (no known cause).
Treatment: Antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine) — non-sedating preferred. Corticosteroids for severe flares. Epinephrine for anaphylaxis associated with severe urticaria.

CONTACT DERMATITIS
Skin inflammation caused by contact with a substance.
Irritant contact dermatitis: From soaps, detergents, chemicals (most common).
Allergic contact dermatitis: Immune reaction to allergen (nickel, latex, poison ivy, fragrances, cosmetics).
Symptoms: Redness, itching, burning, blisters, crusting confined to area of contact.
Treatment: Identify and avoid the trigger. Corticosteroid cream. Antihistamines for itching. Cool compresses.

SKIN INFECTIONS

CELLULITIS
Bacterial infection of the deeper layers of skin (dermis and subcutaneous tissue). Usually Streptococcus pyogenes or Staphylococcus aureus.
Symptoms: Area of red, swollen, warm, tender skin that may spread rapidly. Fever and chills in more serious cases. Red streaks spreading from the area (lymphangitis) is a sign of spreading infection.
Treatment: Antibiotics — flucloxacillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate, or cephalexin orally. IV antibiotics for severe/spreading cases. Elevate the affected limb. Mark the border to monitor spread.

IMPETIGO
Highly contagious bacterial skin infection. Most common in children. Caused by Staphylococcus aureus or Streptococcus.
Non-bullous impetigo: Red sores that burst, leak fluid, and crust over (honey-colored crust). Usually around nose and mouth.
Bullous impetigo: Fluid-filled blisters that burst and leave a scaly edge.
Treatment: Topical fusidic acid or mupirocin (for localized infection). Oral flucloxacillin or amoxicillin-clavulanate for widespread infection.

SHINGLES (HERPES ZOSTER)
Reactivation of the chickenpox virus (varicella-zoster) that has remained dormant in nerve tissue.
Risk factors: older age (>50), weakened immune system, stress.
Symptoms: Pain, burning, or tingling in a localized area. Rash of fluid-filled blisters on one side of the body (following a nerve path/dermatome). Fever, headache, fatigue.
Complications: Post-herpetic neuralgia (chronic nerve pain after rash heals), eye involvement (if near eye — sight-threatening), bacterial infection of blisters.
Treatment: Start antiviral medication (acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir) within 72 hours of rash onset for best effect. Pain management: paracetamol, NSAIDs, tricyclic antidepressants, gabapentin for nerve pain.
Shingles vaccine (Shingrix): Recommended for adults 50+ even if previously had shingles.

SKIN CANCER — EARLY WARNING SIGNS (ABCDE RULE)
A — Asymmetry: One half of the mole doesn't match the other.
B — Border: Irregular, ragged, notched, or blurred edges.
C — Color: Variation in color (different shades of brown, black, red, white, or blue).
D — Diameter: Larger than 6mm (about the size of a pencil eraser). However, melanomas can be smaller.
E — Evolving: Any change in size, shape, color, or new symptom (bleeding, itching, crusting).

Report any changing skin lesion to a dermatologist promptly.

SUN PROTECTION (PREVENTS SKIN CANCER AND PREMATURE AGING)
Use broad-spectrum (UVA + UVB) sunscreen SPF 30+ daily. Apply 15–30 minutes before sun exposure. Reapply every 2 hours. Wear protective clothing, hat, sunglasses. Avoid sun between 10am–4pm (peak intensity). No tanning beds.
