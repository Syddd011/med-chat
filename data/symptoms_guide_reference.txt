SYMPTOMS GUIDE — WHAT COMMON SYMPTOMS MAY INDICATE

IMPORTANT: This guide is for general educational information only. Symptoms can have many causes. Always consult a licensed doctor for a proper diagnosis. If you have a medical emergency, call 108 (India), 911 (USA), 999 (UK), or 112 (Global).

CHEST PAIN

Possible causes:
- Heart attack (myocardial infarction): Pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain in the center or left side of chest. May radiate to arm, jaw, back, neck. Associated with shortness of breath, sweating, nausea. EMERGENCY — call 108/911 immediately.
- Angina: Similar to heart attack but usually triggered by exertion/stress and relieved by rest or nitrates.
- Pulmonary embolism: Sudden sharp chest pain, shortness of breath, rapid heartbeat, coughing blood. EMERGENCY.
- Pericarditis: Sharp chest pain that improves leaning forward. May follow viral illness.
- Aortic dissection: Sudden, severe tearing or ripping chest/back pain. EMERGENCY.
- Gastroesophageal reflux (GERD/acid reflux): Burning chest pain (heartburn) after eating, worse lying down. Relieved by antacids.
- Costochondritis: Sharp pain at junction of rib and breastbone, worsens with movement/touch.
- Anxiety/panic attack: Chest tightness, palpitations, shortness of breath, dizziness.
- Pneumonia or pleurisy: Chest pain that worsens with breathing or coughing. Associated with fever.
- Esophageal spasm: Severe chest pain not related to exercise, may mimic heart attack.

Seek emergency care if: chest pain is accompanied by shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, arm or jaw pain, or if severe and sudden.

SHORTNESS OF BREATH (DYSPNEA)

Possible causes:
- Asthma attack: Wheezing, coughing, chest tightness. Relieved by bronchodilator inhaler.
- COPD exacerbation: Worsening breathlessness in known COPD patient. Increased cough and phlegm.
- Heart failure: Breathlessness worsening progressively, worse lying flat (orthopnea), leg swelling.
- Pneumonia: Breathlessness with fever, cough, chest pain.
- Pulmonary embolism (blood clot in lungs): Sudden breathlessness, sharp chest pain, rapid heartbeat. EMERGENCY.
- Anemia: Breathlessness with fatigue, pallor, palpitations.
- Anxiety/panic attack: Hyperventilation, chest tightness, sense of doom.
- Pneumothorax (collapsed lung): Sudden sharp chest pain and shortness of breath. EMERGENCY.
- COVID-19 pneumonia or other viral pneumonia.

Seek emergency care if: Breathing is severely compromised, lips or fingernails are turning blue, unable to complete sentences.

HEADACHE

Possible causes:
- Tension headache (most common): Dull, aching, band-like pressure around the head. Caused by stress, poor posture, eye strain, dehydration.
- Migraine: Throbbing unilateral (one-sided) headache, moderate-severe intensity, associated with nausea, vomiting, sensitivity to light and sound. May have aura (visual disturbances). Lasts 4–72 hours.
- Cluster headache: Severe, one-sided headache around the eye. Very intense. Associated with eye redness, tearing, runny nose. Occurs in clusters over weeks.
- Sinusitis: Pressure/pain in forehead, cheeks, around eyes. Associated with congestion and green/yellow nasal discharge.
- Hypertension: Rarely causes headache (only with severely elevated BP — hypertensive crisis).
- Medication overuse headache: Rebound headache from overusing pain medications.
- Meningitis: Severe headache, stiff neck, fever, sensitivity to light, rash (non-blanching in meningococcal disease). EMERGENCY.
- Subarachnoid hemorrhage (brain bleed): "Thunderclap headache" — sudden, extremely severe headache, worst of life. EMERGENCY.
- Brain tumor: Gradually worsening headache, worse in morning or lying down, associated with neurological symptoms.

RED FLAG HEADACHE SYMPTOMS (seek emergency care immediately):
- Worst headache of your life / sudden severe onset
- Headache with fever, stiff neck, rash
- Headache after head injury
- Headache with neurological symptoms (weakness, vision changes, speech problems, confusion)
- New headache pattern in someone over 50

FATIGUE AND LOW ENERGY

Possible causes:
- Sleep deprivation: most common cause. Address sleep habits first.
- Anemia (low iron or B12): Fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, palpitations, dizziness.
- Hypothyroidism: Fatigue with weight gain, cold intolerance, dry skin, constipation, depression.
- Diabetes: Fatigue from uncontrolled blood sugar (both high and low).
- Depression: Fatigue with persistent sadness, loss of interest, sleep changes.
- Chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS): Profound fatigue not improved by rest, post-exertional malaise.
- Heart disease or heart failure: Fatigue with exertional breathlessness.
- Kidney disease or liver disease: Fatigue with other systemic symptoms.
- Cancer: Unexplained persistent fatigue (especially with weight loss, night sweats, lumps).
- Infections: Fatigue following viral illness (post-viral fatigue — common after COVID-19, Epstein-Barr).
- Medications: Many medications cause fatigue as a side effect.
- Nutritional deficiencies: Vitamin D, B12, iron, magnesium deficiency.

NAUSEA AND VOMITING

Possible causes:
- Gastroenteritis (stomach flu): Vomiting and diarrhea with or without fever. Usually self-limiting viral illness.
- Food poisoning: Nausea and vomiting within hours of eating contaminated food.
- Motion sickness: Nausea with travel (car, boat, plane).
- Pregnancy (morning sickness): Nausea and vomiting in first trimester.
- Migraine: Often accompanied by nausea and vomiting.
- Medication side effects: Many medications cause nausea (antibiotics, metformin, NSAIDs, chemotherapy).
- GERD/acid reflux: Regurgitation and nausea after meals.
- Appendicitis: Nausea with right lower abdominal pain that worsens, fever. Needs urgent evaluation.
- Bowel obstruction: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, inability to pass gas/stool. EMERGENCY.
- Diabetic ketoacidosis: Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain in diabetics with very high blood sugar. EMERGENCY.
- Head injury or brain conditions: Vomiting with headache, confusion.
- Meningitis.

Seek care if: vomiting persists more than 24 hours, unable to keep any fluids down (dehydration risk), blood in vomit, severe abdominal pain, signs of dehydration (dark urine, no urination, extreme thirst, dizziness).

DIZZINESS AND VERTIGO

Possible causes:
- Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV): Sudden spinning sensation with head movement (e.g. rolling over in bed, looking up). Most common cause of vertigo. Treated with Epley maneuver.
- Labyrinthitis or vestibular neuritis: Sudden severe vertigo, nausea, vomiting following a viral illness.
- Meniere's disease: Episodes of vertigo lasting 20min–several hours, associated with hearing loss, tinnitus (ringing), ear fullness.
- Orthostatic hypotension: Lightheadedness/dizziness on standing up suddenly. Blood pressure drops temporarily.
- Anemia: Lightheadedness with pallor and fatigue.
- Dehydration: Lightheadedness, especially in hot weather or after poor fluid intake.
- Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia): Dizziness, shakiness, sweating — especially in diabetics.
- Stroke or TIA: Sudden vertigo with other neurological symptoms (weakness, speech difficulty, vision change). EMERGENCY.
- Anxiety: Dizziness associated with anxiety episodes.
- Medication side effects: Blood pressure medications, sedatives.

BACK PAIN

Possible causes:
- Muscle strain (most common): Dull aching pain, often after lifting or twisting. Improves with rest and NSAIDs.
- Disc herniation (slipped disc): Pain radiating down the leg (sciatica), numbness or tingling. Confirmed by MRI.
- Sciatica: Sharp, shooting pain radiating from lower back through buttock into the leg along the sciatic nerve.
- Osteoarthritis of the spine (spondylosis): Stiffness and pain, worse in morning or after activity.
- Ankylosing spondylitis: Chronic back pain in young males, worse in morning, improves with exercise.
- Kidney stones or kidney infection: Flank pain (one-sided back pain below ribs), radiating to groin, associated with urinary symptoms.
- Osteoporosis with compression fracture: Sudden back pain after minor fall in older adults/postmenopausal women.
- Cancer metastasis to spine: Persistent, progressive back pain not relieved by rest. Night pain. Unexplained weight loss.

RED FLAGS for back pain (seek urgent evaluation):
- Pain after trauma
- Night pain or pain at rest
- Associated with fever or weight loss
- Bladder or bowel dysfunction (cauda equina syndrome — emergency)
- Neurological symptoms (weakness, numbness in legs)
- Age over 50 with new onset back pain

ABDOMINAL PAIN

Location clues:
- Right upper quadrant: Liver, gallbladder, right kidney. Gallstones — sharp pain after fatty meal radiating to right shoulder.
- Epigastric (central upper): Stomach, pancreas, heart. Gastritis, peptic ulcer, GERD, pancreatitis, heart attack.
- Left upper quadrant: Spleen, left kidney, stomach.
- Periumbilical (around navel): Small intestine, early appendicitis, aortic aneurysm.
- Right lower quadrant: Appendix, right ovary. Appendicitis — pain that starts around navel and moves to right lower, worsens with movement, fever.
- Left lower quadrant: Sigmoid colon, left ovary. Diverticulitis — fever, left-sided pain in older adults.
- Lower central (suprapubic): Bladder, uterus, ovaries. UTI, ovarian cyst/torsion, ectopic pregnancy.
- Generalized: Gastroenteritis, IBS, bowel obstruction, peritonitis.

Seek emergency care if: Sudden, severe pain ("worst ever"), pain with rigidity of abdomen (board-like rigidity), pain with fever and vomiting, suspected appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy.

WEIGHT LOSS (UNEXPLAINED)

Losing more than 5% of body weight within 6–12 months without trying.
Possible causes:
- Diabetes mellitus (especially type 1 and uncontrolled type 2)
- Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid)
- Cancer (any type — "B symptoms": weight loss, night sweats, fever)
- Tuberculosis or chronic infection
- HIV/AIDS
- Chronic malabsorption (Crohn's disease, celiac disease)
- Heart failure, kidney disease, liver cirrhosis (advanced)
- Depression or anxiety (reduced appetite)
- Medication side effects
- Eating disorders

ALWAYS see a doctor for unexplained weight loss — it is an important warning sign.

FEVER

Temperature above 38°C (100.4°F). Common causes:
- Infections (most common): viral (cold, flu, COVID-19), bacterial (UTI, pneumonia, TB, typhoid), parasitic (malaria, dengue).
- Inflammatory conditions: rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, inflammatory bowel disease.
- Drug fever: reaction to medications.
- Cancer: lymphoma, leukemia (fever without obvious infection source).
- Malaria: Cyclic fever with chills, sweating — common in India.
- Dengue: Fever with severe headache, pain behind eyes, joint/muscle pain, rash. Platelet count drops.
- Typhoid: Sustained fever for more than a week, headache, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhea.

Seek emergency care if: Fever above 40°C in adults, fever in infant under 3 months, fever with severe headache and stiff neck (meningitis), fever with difficulty breathing, fever with rash, fever with altered consciousness.
