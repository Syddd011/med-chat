PEDIATRIC HEALTH — COMPREHENSIVE MEDICAL REFERENCE

FEVER IN CHILDREN

DEFINITION
Fever is a body temperature above 38°C (100.4°F). It is usually a sign that the immune system is fighting an infection — most commonly viral.

TEMPERATURE THRESHOLDS
- Below 38°C (100.4°F): Normal
- 38–38.9°C (100.4–102°F): Low-grade fever — monitor
- 39–40°C (102.2–104°F): High fever — treat symptoms
- Above 40°C (104°F): Very high fever — seek medical care promptly
- Any fever in a baby under 3 months: Emergency — go to ER immediately

TREATMENT
Keep child hydrated (offer fluids frequently). Dress lightly. Paracetamol (acetaminophen): Dose based on weight — typically 10–15 mg/kg every 4–6 hours. Do NOT give aspirin to children under 16. Ibuprofen: Safe for children over 6 months. Dose based on weight. Do NOT alternate paracetamol and ibuprofen without medical advice. Tepid sponging can help bring down temperature (use lukewarm, not cold water).

WHEN TO SEE A DOCTOR IMMEDIATELY
Baby under 3 months with any fever. Child with fever above 40°C. Fever lasting more than 3 days. Fever with severe headache, stiff neck, rash, difficulty breathing, confusion, seizure, persistent vomiting, signs of dehydration.

FEBRILE SEIZURES
Seizures triggered by rapid rise in temperature. Affect 2–4% of children aged 6 months to 5 years. Usually brief (less than 5 minutes) and not dangerous. Place child on their side, clear area, do not restrain or put anything in mouth. Seek medical evaluation after a febrile seizure, especially if it is the first one.

COMMON CHILDHOOD ILLNESSES

UPPER RESPIRATORY INFECTIONS (COMMON COLD)
Most common illness in children. Caused by viruses (rhinovirus most common). Symptoms: runny/stuffy nose, sneezing, mild fever, cough, sore throat. Treatment: supportive care only — rest, fluids, saline nasal drops, honey for cough in children over 1 year. Do NOT give cough/cold medicines to children under 4 years.

CROUP
Viral infection causing swelling in the larynx/trachea. Characteristic barking (seal-like) cough, hoarseness, stridor (noisy breathing when inhaling). More common at night. Usually in children aged 6 months to 3 years.
Mild croup: Expose to cool night air or steam from shower. Keep child calm (crying worsens symptoms).
Moderate-severe croup: Seek emergency care. Treated with corticosteroids (dexamethasone) and nebulized epinephrine in severe cases.

BRONCHIOLITIS
Viral infection (usually RSV) of small airways. Common in infants under 2 years, peak at age 3–6 months. Symptoms: runny nose, cough, rapid breathing, wheezing, feeding difficulties. Treatment: supportive — fluids, positioning, nasal suctioning. No antibiotics (viral). Hospitalization if breathing is significantly compromised.

OTITIS MEDIA (EAR INFECTION)
Very common in young children. Symptoms: ear pain (child tugging at ear), fussiness, fever, hearing difficulty, fluid draining from ear. Most resolve without antibiotics. Watchful waiting for 48–72 hours in mild cases for children over 2. Amoxicillin is first-line antibiotic if needed.

GASTROENTERITIS (STOMACH FLU)
Viral or bacterial infection causing vomiting and diarrhea. Most important treatment: PREVENT DEHYDRATION. Give oral rehydration solution (ORS) in small, frequent amounts. Do NOT give sports drinks, fruit juice, or soda. Signs of dehydration in children: no tears, dry mouth, sunken eyes, no urination for 8+ hours, extreme lethargy. Seek emergency care for severe dehydration.

CHICKENPOX (VARICELLA)
Highly contagious viral infection. Presents as itchy red spots that progress to fluid-filled blisters, then crust over. Spreads 1–2 days before rash appears until all blisters crust over. Treatment: symptomatic (antihistamines for itching, calamine lotion, paracetamol for fever — NOT aspirin). Antiviral (acyclovir) for high-risk groups. Vaccine available (part of childhood schedule).

HAND, FOOT AND MOUTH DISEASE
Common viral illness in young children. Causes sores in mouth and rash/blisters on hands, feet, buttocks. Mildly contagious. Spreads through saliva, blister fluid, stool. Treatment: symptomatic — fluids, pain relief. Ensure good hydration as mouth sores make swallowing painful.

DEVELOPMENTAL MILESTONES

2 MONTHS: Smiles at people, follows things with eyes, begins to make cooing sounds.
4 MONTHS: Holds head steady, pushes up on arms, babbles.
6 MONTHS: Rolls over, sits with support, responds to sounds, recognizes familiar faces.
9 MONTHS: Stands while holding on, plays peek-a-boo, says "mama/dada" (non-specifically).
12 MONTHS: Pulls to stand, walks with support, says 1–2 words, waves bye-bye.
18 MONTHS: Walks independently, says 8–10 words, points to things. Red flags: not walking by 18 months, not saying any words.
24 MONTHS: Runs, uses 2-word phrases ("more milk"), vocabulary of 50+ words. Red flag: not using 2-word phrases.
3 YEARS: Speaks in sentences, climbs stairs alternating feet, plays with other children.
4 YEARS: Tells stories, hops on one foot, draws a person with 4 body parts.
5 YEARS: Counts 10+ objects, writes name, dresses/undresses alone.

WHEN TO SEE A DOCTOR FOR DEVELOPMENTAL CONCERNS
No babbling by 12 months, no pointing or waving by 12 months, no words by 16 months, no two-word phrases by 24 months, loss of previously acquired language or social skills at any age, not making eye contact, not responding to their name by 12 months.

ADHD (ATTENTION-DEFICIT/HYPERACTIVITY DISORDER)
Neurodevelopmental disorder. Median age of diagnosis: 7 years.
Types: Predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, combined presentation.
Symptoms (inattentive): short attention span, easily distracted, forgetful, loses things, difficulty finishing tasks.
Symptoms (hyperactive/impulsive): fidgeting, inability to sit still, talking excessively, interrupting, acting without thinking.
Treatment: Behavioral therapy (first-line for children under 6), medications (methylphenidate, amphetamine salts for children 6+), parent training, school accommodations.

AUTISM SPECTRUM DISORDER (ASD)
A neurodevelopmental condition affecting social interaction, communication, and behavior.
Early warning signs (12–18 months): not pointing, not making eye contact, not sharing interest with others, not responding to name, no words by 16 months, stereotyped movements (hand-flapping, rocking).
Diagnosis: developmental assessment by a specialist (pediatric neurologist, child psychiatrist). No single blood test exists.
Treatment: Early intensive behavioral intervention (ABA therapy), speech therapy, occupational therapy, educational support. Early diagnosis and intervention significantly improve outcomes.

CHILDHOOD NUTRITION ESSENTIALS
Breastfeeding: Exclusive for 6 months. Continue alongside solid foods until 2 years.
Solid food introduction: Around 6 months. Introduce one new food at a time and wait 3–5 days to watch for allergies.
Iron-rich foods: Essential after 6 months (infant iron stores deplete). Pureed meats, iron-fortified cereals, lentils, beans.
Calcium: Growing bones need calcium — dairy, fortified plant milks, green leafy vegetables.
Avoid giving honey to children under 1 year (risk of botulism).
Avoid whole grapes, nuts, large chunks of raw vegetables, popcorn before age 4 (choking risk).
Limit fruit juice — no juice before 1 year, limit to 4 oz/day for 1–3 years.
Vitamin D: Supplement 400 IU/day for all breastfed infants from birth.

DIAPER RASH
Redness and irritation in the diaper area. Caused by prolonged exposure to wet/soiled diaper, friction, or yeast infection (Candida).
Treatment: Change diapers frequently, clean area gently with warm water, air dry, apply zinc oxide barrier cream (Sudocrem, Desitin). For persistent yeast rash (bright red with satellite spots): antifungal cream (clotrimazole).

CHILDHOOD OBESITY
BMI above 95th percentile for age and sex. Increasing concern globally. Linked to type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, sleep apnea, orthopedic problems, and psychosocial issues. Management: family-based lifestyle change, healthy diet, physical activity, limiting screen time. Rarely medications in adolescents.

SLEEP RECOMMENDATIONS FOR CHILDREN
Newborn (0–3 months): 14–17 hours/day
Infant (4–11 months): 12–15 hours/day
Toddler (1–2 years): 11–14 hours/day
Preschool (3–5 years): 10–13 hours/day
School-age (6–12 years): 9–11 hours/day
Teenager (13–18 years): 8–10 hours/day
